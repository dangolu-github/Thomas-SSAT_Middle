Knowledge Foundation · Event Chain

历史阅读不要求先记住所有事实。先问四件事：What changed? Who acted? Why? What followed?

1763–1765New pressure

Britain sought greater revenue and control; Parliament imposed new measures on the colonies.

1774Collective response

Colonial delegates met and organized petitions, boycotts, and shared objections.

1775Armed conflict

Fighting began at Lexington and Concord while colonists still disagreed about the final goal.

1776Public declaration

Congress adopted a document explaining why political separation had become necessary.

Reading boundary: these events are background for understanding a social-studies passage. The key skill is to connect pressure → response → conflict → public justification.

The Declaration as an argument

1 · IntroductionNames the need to explain separation.
2 · PrinciplesStates ideas about rights and legitimate government.
3 · EvidenceLists grievances and failed requests for redress.
4 · ResolutionAnnounces independence and the powers of a separate state.

Bridge Paragraph

When a distant government claimed authority to impose new taxes, many colonists argued that consent and representation were missing. They signed a petition to describe each grievance and ask for redress. As officials refused to change course, resistance grew. Some colonists began to question whether British rule remained legitimate. Eventually, supporters of independence argued that the colonies should abolish their former political ties, seek an alliance with foreign powers, and claim sovereignty as separate states.

Reading Transfer · From Grievance to Declaration

Read → Map → Limit → Answer. First map each paragraph's job. Then use only the evidence required by the question.

In 1763, most colonists in British North America still considered themselves members of the British Empire. After a costly war, however, Britain sought more revenue and tighter control. Parliament imposed measures such as the Stamp Act, while many colonists argued that taxation without representation violated the proper limits of government. Their resistance did not begin as a single demand for independence. At first, boycotts and petitions asked British leaders to correct particular grievances.

By 1774, delegates from several colonies were meeting to organize a collective response. Fighting at Lexington and Concord began in 1775, yet colonial opinion remained divided. Some people hoped for reconciliation; others increasingly believed that British authority was no longer legitimate. As petitions failed and armed conflict widened, complete separation became a more believable course of action.

In July 1776, the Continental Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence. The document did more than announce a decision. Its introduction explained why separation required a public justification. Its preamble stated broad principles, including the idea that government rests on the consent of the governed. A long list of grievances then served as evidence that British rule had violated those principles. Finally, the resolution declared the colonies free and independent states with the power to form alliances and govern themselves.

The order of these parts reveals the document's public purpose. The Declaration addressed the king, the colonists, and audiences beyond North America. It sought to justify resistance, unite supporters, and make foreign assistance more likely. A reader who follows the sequence—principle, evidence, conclusion—can understand the argument without memorizing every date or complaint.
